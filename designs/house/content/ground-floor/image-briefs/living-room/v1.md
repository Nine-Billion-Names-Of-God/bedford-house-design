## 1. What is wrong with 01 and 02

The problem is sameness. The entrance-hallway, reception-room and living-room iterations all use one formula:

- traditional rug (Persian-style in the hall and reception, kilim in Living Room 02)
- gilt-framed landscape oil paintings
- brass lamp with a linen or pleated shade
- dark antique or walnut wood, with turned or carved legs
- the same cushion trio (rust velvet, olive, flax lumbar with a stripe) and a cream fringed throw

Living Room 02 answered "older, more character" by making the TV area a **third period room**. Specifically:

- **No transition to the kitchen.** The view towards the kitchen shows a Victorian sitting room with a sleek charcoal-and-walnut kitchen behind it, and nothing joins the two.
- **Carved oak coffer.** It is rustic and country-house in style. It also suits a TV poorly: it is deep, has a lifting lid and gives no ventilation or cable access.
- **Turned-leg, barley-style coffee table.** This is the front-of-house vocabulary again.
- **Kilim rug.** It is too large and too busy, and its brick and blue colours compete with the red hat, the red glass and the shelf collection.
- **Brass table lamp with a linen shade.** It is the same lamp as in the reception.
- **Sofa, cushions and throw.** They are identical in 01 and 02: generic taupe, the cushion trio and the fringed throw.

**What 01 and 02 got right, to keep:**

- the TV mounted on its current wall
- no floor-standing black unit
- a straight sofa with no chaise
- the user's actual shelf collection (02)
- the red hat above the ladder shelf
- the user's clock back above the sofa
- the monstera

- the real raised dog bed

## 2. The idea: a mid-century hinge

The agreed philosophy (live brief §7.1) has a period front, a contemporary rear, and **the TV area as the hinge that borrows from both**.

The front of the house belongs to the 1880s and the kitchen to the 2020s. The hinge takes **the era in between: mid-century (about 1955–1975)**. This works for three reasons:

- **It meets the user's feedback.** It is genuinely older and full of character, with real wood and patina, which answers the rejection of the slick floating cabinet.
- **It speaks the kitchen's language.** Clean lines, dark walnut, black metal, leather and rounded shapes match the walnut island, charcoal units, black steel screen and rust velvet stools.
- **It already exists in the house.** The green armchairs with bentwood arms in the front lounge are mid-century in shape. The user's world objects (the Vietnamese hat, seated figure, carved figures and the dining-room mask) suit a mid-century collector's room better than an English country one.

The test is the **towards-kitchen view**. The eye should travel from panelled walls to walnut and leather, then to the walnut island and charcoal units, with each step related to the one before.

**Shared thread.** Terracotta or rust, warm timber and natural wool appear in every zone. In this room terracotta comes from the rug accents and the ceramic lamp. Charcoal comes from the rug and the stone tabletop and links forward to the kitchen units.

## 8. Notes to record in `design.md`

- **Leather and the dog.** Semi-aniline or pigmented leather resists claws better than full aniline, which marks but develops a patina. Fabric alternative if leather is not wanted: deep olive or ink-blue wool weave, keeping the same shape.
- **Sideboard.** Check its depth against the walkway between the sofa and the TV. Set the TV height from the seated eye-line on the chosen sofa. Run cables from the back of the TV into the sideboard.
- **Rug.** Measure the size with a floor template. Use underlay suitable for the underfloor heating.
- **Clocks.** There is a second clock on the kitchen wall by the hall door. Both are visible in the towards-kitchen view; note this for the kitchen stage rather than changing it now.
- **Concepts only.** All new pieces are concepts, not sourced products. For the sideboard and lamp, genuine vintage pieces are preferred to new reproductions.
