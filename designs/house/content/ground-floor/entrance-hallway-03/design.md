## 2. Design reasoning

### A brown Persian-style runner can work here

The bare oak stair now provides a large quiet surface, giving a patterned hall rug more room to contribute character. Brown, camel and tobacco relate to the oak, dark console and handrail; small traces of faded rust connect to the wider ground-floor palette without making the rug predominantly red.

The tiles and rug have different pattern scales: the floor has crisp geometric stars, while the rug has a softer, more irregular traditional floral drawing. Leaving a continuous band of tile on both sides makes that relationship legible. A softly worn, matte surface keeps the rug from looking like a strong new graphic stripe.

For this direction, favour a mellow brown ground with camel and ivory detail, small rust and olive accents, and a defined brown border. Avoid treating “Persian” as a requirement for a bright red field or an oversized central medallion. The image is a Persian-style concept, not evidence of a particular rug's origin, age or construction.

### Put the character into a few substantial choices

The main transformation is the combination of rug and painting group. Their traditional detail gives the hall a more collected atmosphere, while the uncovered oak remains prominent. Small console objects then reinforce that direction rather than carrying the whole design on their own.

The aged gilt frames have a different role from the existing dark hammered mirror. They introduce a warmer decorative finish that relates to the brass door furniture. Keeping all three paintings in a similar brown/olive/ochre tonal family gives the group coherence without making it look like three copies of one print.

### Make the lighting part of the decoration

A pleated olive shade and a more decorative brass lamp create a stronger evening focal point than the plain oatmeal shade in 02. The green echoes the stained glass and plant; the rust edging introduces a small warm detail. The lamp remains small enough for the retained demi-lune table.

## 6. Further design ideas to explore

These are additional options, **not installed elements shown in the mockups** and not approved changes.

1. **Choose art with a personal connection.** An older local architectural view, a landscape of somewhere meaningful, and a small traditional oil could form the three-picture group. Matching their tonal weight matters more than buying a matching set. This would give the stair more identity than generic prints.
2. **Trial a discreet picture light.** One small rechargeable aged-brass light over the central painting could make the gallery an evening feature without chasing cables into the wall. Check projection and the available wall height first; it is not rendered in this version.
3. **Use actual objects with a history on the console.** A family miniature, inherited porcelain dish or a pair of small books you already own would make the layered arrangement feel more personal. There is no need to buy every accessory shown.
4. **Compare two brown rug samples in the room.** The rendered rug is fairly dark and richly patterned. A lighter camel/tobacco example with more faded ivory could give a softer transition to the grey tiles if this one feels too heavy. Keep both examples traditionally patterned; the rejected plain strip is not the fallback.
5. **Give the evening lighting its own setting.** Use the small table lamp as the main entrance glow when strong overhead light is unnecessary, with the existing pendants providing the rest. Judge a fabric shade in the actual room before buying: a dark green exterior and pale lining should still provide useful light.

## 7. What stays fixed

Wall, ceiling and joinery paint; the door and stained glass; fanlight; ribbed-glass pendants; geometric star tiles; cornice and all stair panelling; cupboards; black steel glazing; oak stair and dark handrail; retained console, mirror and dog hamper. No new bench, bulky floor plant, storage cabinet or umbrella stand is added to the narrow route.

The rug, gallery and styled table provide the change in character. Extra objects are concentrated on the existing tabletop and wall, where they can contribute without filling the walking route.
